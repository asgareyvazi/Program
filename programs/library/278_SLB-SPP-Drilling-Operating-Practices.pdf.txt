What is a STUCK PIPE?

 When the drillstring is unable to
  freely move up, move down,
  rotate, and/or circulate.
 Down hole operations have been
  suspended because forces
  prevent moving the drill string,
  liner, casing, or wire line from its
  current position



  Sticking can occur during drilling,
  tripping, while making a
 connection, logging, running
 casing and completion, back
 reaming or during any kind of
 operation



HOW to get STUCK?

   Lack of planning
   Poor drilling practices
   Not listening to the well
    Drilling faster than you can
      clean the hole
   Not monitoring torque & drag
   Not following the plan
   Back reaming
   Surface equipment (pumps)
   Rig capacity
   Inadequate or poor mud
      properties
    Drilling in a depleted zone
   Poor or no handover
   Incorrect reaction
   Long connection time
   Poor housekeeping

        Hole cleaning


Causes
   Drilled cuttings not transported out of
    hole forming cutting beds
   Insufficient rotation
   Poor fluid properties
   Lack of Patience
  Low annular velocity

Occurrence
  Most common at 30–60°inclination
  The well is drilled faster than the hole
     is cleaned

Rig site warnings
   Increasing TnD while drilling
  Reduced cuttings returns at shakers
   Increasing pump pressure/ ECDs
   Increasing over pulls while POOH
   Over pulls inside casing /after
    connection

Preventive actions
   Maximize rotation and reciprocation-
    cuttings bed agitation
   Ensure circulation times are adequate
  Match ROP to hole cleaning
   Maximize annular velocity
   Monitor the cuttings returns at shakers
   High/low weighted pills can promote
    cuttings agitation
   Consult the hole cleaning calculations
   Optimize mud properties- increase YP
     in near vertical wells
   Consider using larger drill pipe

   Unconsolidated Formations


Causes
   Poor bonding between particles in the
    formation
    Little or no mudcake present
  No formation support from hydrostatic
    overbalance
  Sand or gravel falling in the hole

Occurrence
  When drilling top hole/ under pressure

Rig site warnings
   Increasing pump pressure due to
    increasing solids in the mud
   Increasing torque and drag
   Overpull on connection
    Fill on bottom in low angle wells
   Shaker blinding leading to losses at
    surface

Preventive actions
   Design the mud to build a low perm
   cake
   Maintain adequate hole cleaning (flow
    rate and viscosity)
   Control drill to maintain adequate hole
    cleaning and to allow time for the filter
   cake to build up
   Avoid unnecessary reaming and back
   reaming
   Clean out hole fill prior to drilling
   ahead
   Consider viscous pills on trips
  Keep BHA to a minimum

        Reactive Shale


Causes
   Water sensitive shale/clays drilled with
    less inhibition than required
   Shale swells into the wellbore and
     restricts annular space
   Poor drilling practices

Occurrence
  More often with WBM, less with OBM
  The reaction is time dependent on
    mud-formation interaction
  More often while tripping and possible
    while drilling

Rig site warnings
   Hydrated or mushy cavings
   Shakers screens blind off, clay balls
   Increase in LGS, filter cake thickness,
   PV, YP, MBT
   Increasing pump pressure
   Circulation restricted or impossible
   Increasing Torque and Drag
   Generally occurs while BHA is passing
    the reactive formation

Preventive actions
   Inhibited mud system or OBM
    Drill and case off as fast as possible
   Regular wiper trips when drilling
  Keep mud properties within specs
   Minimize the BHA Length
  When using WBM, monitor MBT
    closely. (MBT determines the amount
    of reactive clay in the mud).

     Over-Pressured Shale
            (Natural & Induced)


Causes
   Natural over-pressured shale due to
    geological phenomena (under-
    compaction, uplift)
   Induced over-pressure shale due to
    hydrostatic overbalance (shale cracks
   and falls into the wellbore)

Occurrence
   Natural: Removal of ECD (swab during
     trip out) or at pumps off
   Induced: after MW reduction, while
     drilling or tripping

Rig site warnings
   Cavings (splintery) at shakers
   Cuttings and cavings are not hydrated
    or mushy
   Ballooning effect
   Increased Torque and Drag
  Gas levels increase
   Circulation restricted or impossible
   Hole fill after trips
  An increase in ROP due to under
    balanced conditions

Preventive actions
   Monitor shale shakers
  Use sufficient mud weight
  Use pore pressure analysis and
    confirm with gas readings
   Plan to minimize hole exposure time
   Avoid reducing mud weight in shale
   Control ECD to minimize ballooning
  Good hole cleaning
    practices

Fractured and Faulted Formations


 Causes
   Rock near fault can be broken into
     small or large pieces that if loose can
           fall into the hole and jam the drillstring
     Drilling Parallel to bedding planes

 Occurrence
    Tectonically active zones
    Fractured limestone or coal
    While drilling or tripping

 Rig site warnings
    Hole fill on connections
    Possible losses or gains (or
     ballooning)
    Splintering cavings
    Fault damaged cavings at shakers
    Sticking can be instantaneous

 Preventive actions
    Plan the well properly to minimize
    exposure
   Keep the annular space as clean as
     possible, avoid overloading
    Monitor and minimize ECD while
      drilling to avoid inducing losses
    Limit the rotary speed and tripping
    speeds across fractured formations
   Wash and ream when running in and
     clean out hole prior to drilling ahead
    Spot acid if stuck in limestone.
      If coal present, reduce ROP and
     control drill the coal section. Minimize
    exposure if possible.

        Stress Imbalance



Causes
   Natural stresses due to earth’s crust
   movement
   Formation fractures and falls into the
    wellbore

Occurrence
   In tectonically stressed areas
   Using incorrect mud weight
   While drilling or tripping

Rig site warnings
   Pack-offs may occur.
   Cavings at the shakers
   Increasing Torque and Drag
     If stuck, circulation is likely to be
    impaired or impossible.
   Increasing volume of returns at the
    shakers relative to the hole volume
     drilled

Preventive actions
  Use offset data to establish optimum
    inclination and azimuth
   Maintain mud weight and ECD within
    planned mud weight window
    accounting for swab during trips
   Plan to case off these formations as
    quickly as possible
     If possible drill these formations in
    smaller hole sizes
   Maintain the hole clean and be
    prepared for increased amount of
    cuttings and cavings

       Junk in the Hole


Causes
  Downhole equipment failure
   Poor rig floor housekeeping
   Inattention / hole cover not installed
  Junk jams the drill string

Occurrence
   Anytime

Rig site warnings
   Missing hand tools / equipment
   Circulation unrestricted
   Metal shavings at shaker
  Junk damage on BHA components
  Sudden erratic torque
    Inability to drill ahead

Preventive actions
   Only use equipment that has passed
    inspection
   Visually inspect all tools before
    running
   Inspect all handling tools regularly,
    especially rig tongs and slips
  Keep the hole covered whenever
    possible. Be careful working around
    the hole when it is open
   Close the blind rams if there is no
   problems with the hole
    Install drill pipe wiper whenever
    possible when tripping in/out
   Practice good housekeeping
     If stuck, jarring can help dislodge the
    junk.

Green Cement & LCM Treatment


Causes
   Circulation attempted with the bottom
     of drill string in uncured soft cement.
   The surge in pressure flash sets the
   cement around the BHA.

Occurrence
  When cleaning out green cement or
   LCM

Rig site warnings
   Increase in pump pressure leading to
     inability to circulate
   Loss of string weight and possible
    reduction in torque
   Soft cement on shakers, discoloration
     of mud

Preventive actions
   Give cement enough time to set and
    estimate the TOC
   Start circulation several joints above
    the TOC and wash down slowly. Do
    not rely on the weight indicator to
   show TOC.
    Drill out cement with low WOB and
    high flow rate
     If using WBM consider pre-treating
   mud with 0.25-0.5 lb/bbl sodium
    bicarbonate before drilling out to
    minimize mud contamination

       Cement Blocks


Causes
   Hard cement chunks fall into the
    borehole and jam the drill string

Occurrence
  When hard cement becomes unstable
           Casing shoe
          Open hole squeeze plugs
           Kick off plugs
   Excessive rathole

Rig site warnings
   Circulation unrestricted
   Large cement fragments at the
    shakers
   Rotation and downward movement
   may be possible.
   Erratic torque

Preventive actions
   Allow sufficient curing time for cement
    before attempting to kick off or drill out.
    Drill the cement with sufficient flow
    rate and rotation and preferably
    controlled ROP
  Ream casing shoe and open hole
    plugs thoroughly before drilling ahead.
   Limit casing rat hole length to minimize
   a source of cement blocks.
  Slow the trip speed down before the
   BHA enters the casing shoe or the
    plug depth

      Differential Sticking


Causes
   High overbalance applies a differential
    sticking force to the drill string contact
    area
  The drill string is in contact with a
   permeable zone

Occurrence
   With a stationary or very slow moving
      drill string
  When a large contact area exists
   between drill string and wellbore
   In a thick filter cake or cutting bed
  When over balance is present across
   permeable formation

Rig site warnings
   Increasing overpull on connections
     after surveys
   Full unrestricted circulation
   High overbalance / losses
   Increasing trends of friction (break off
    torque) / difficult to rotate after
    connections

Preventive actions
-   Well design
   Optimize casing setting depths to
    minimize overbalance across potential
    sticking zones
   Design for adequate overbalance for
    hole stability and well control
   Design BHA and drillstring to minimize
    contact area

      Differential Sticking


Preventive actions
-
-  Mud system
  Use OBM where possible. Keep fluid
    loss to a minimum
  Use bridging agents such as sized
   CaCO3 and asphalts/gilsonite
   Maintain a low concentration of LGS.

-   Operations
  Keep the string moving. Pre-plan to
    minimize operational downtime
    (connection, surveys, repairs)
   Consider rotating the drillstring during
     drilling, tripping and connections while
   BHA is in a high risk zone (caution
    required)

-   Wellbore contact
   Minimize BHA length
   Maximize standoff, no more than 2 DC
    length unstabilized
   Prefer HWDP over DC’s
   Avoid DC’s above 30˚ inclination
   Avoid flush connections

       Ledges & Doglegs


Causes
  Ledges at interface of hard/soft
    formation
   High dogleg (BHA jammed in dogleg /
    stab blades or upset stuck under ledge)

Occurrence
   Hard/soft interbedded layers
   Faulted/fractured formation
   Frequent angle/direction change
   Drilling/sliding with PDM

Rig site warnings
  Sudden erratic over pull or set down
   Problems reoccurring at same depths
    (also after circulation)
   Full circulation is possible

Preventive action
   Running a packed hole assembly
   Minimize excessive directional changes
     in formations likely to produce ledges
   Consider reaming trips
   Annotate ledges on survey log
   Survey with sufficient frequency.
  Reduce trip speeds across ledge zone
   Avoid prolonged circulation across soft
    interbedded formations
   Limit initial set down weight to less than
   50% of down drag to minimize
   momentum effects when running into a
     tight zone
   Start directional work away from shoe
   Consider RSS assemblies
   Optimize sliding and
 rotating based on
 lithology

      Under gauge hole


Causes
   Hard abrasive rock wears bit gauge
     If RIH fast without reaming the new bit
   can jam in the under gauge section

Occurrence
   After running a new bit
   Running PDC after roller cone
   After BHA changes, while RIH
    Drilling abrasive formation
   After coring

Rig site warnings
   Pulled bit or stabs are under gauge
  Sudden set down weight
   Circulation is free or slightly restricted
   Bit stuck near the bottom of the hole or
    at the top of a cored section

Preventive action
  Use gauge protection in bits and stabs
   Record tight spots while tripping in /out
  Ream suspected under gauge section
  Slow the trip speed down before the
   BHA enters a suspected under gauge
   zone
   Consider the use of roller reamers,
    watermelon mill
   Always gauge all BHA components
    both when running in and pulling out of
    the hole
  Use spiral blade stabilizers instead of
    straight blade stabilizers

      Mobile formation


Causes
   Mobile formation behaves in a plastic
   manner, deforming under pressure
   Overburden weight squeezes
    formation out because the mud weight
     is not sufficient

Occurrence
   Salt or plastic shale
  Most likely encountered during a trip
    out

Rig site warnings
   Over pull when moving up, takes
    weight when running in
   Sticking occurs with BHA at mobile
    formation depth
   Restricted circulation with BHA at
    mobile formation depth

Preventive action
   Maintain sufficient mud weight.
   Select an appropriate mud system
   With mobile salts consider using an
    under-saturated mud system to allow a
    controlled washout (consider using
    water pills to prevent creeping)
   Plan frequent reaming/wiper trips for
     this section of the hole
   Consider bicentric PDC bits or
    concentric reamers
   Minimize the open hole exposure time
    of these formations
  Slow trip speed before BHA enters the
    suspected area

           Stiff assembly


Cause
    Stiffer assembly run into a previously
     drilled hole resulting in stuck

Occurrence
  When doglegs are present
   With different BHA and stabs design
  Most likely when tripping in

Rig site warnings
   Erratic over pull / set down and torque
   Full circulation is possible

Preventive action
   Consider running same or similar BHA
    configurations
   Record previously noticed tight spots
   and run slowly into these sections.
  Slow the trip speed down before the
   new BHA enters a problematic zone
   Consider reaming tight spots with
     sufficient flow rate and rotation

        Key seating


Cause
    Drill pipe rotating against wellbore wall
    creates a groove or “key seat”
   Tool joint or BHA components are
    pulled and jammed into the key seat

Occurrence
   Abrupt change in angle or direction
   After long drilling hours with no wiper
     trips in dogleg section
   While tripping out

Rig site warnings
   Occurs only while POOH.
  Sudden over pull as BHA reaches
    dogleg depth.
   Unrestricted circulation
   Free string movement below key seat
    depth is usually possible if not already
    stuck in key seat
   Cyclic over pull at tool joint intervals on
     trips

Preventive action
   Minimize Side forces
   Minimize dogleg severity especially in
    S-type wells
   Perform reaming and/or wiper trips if a
    high dogleg is present
   Consider running reamers or a key
    seat wiper if a key seat is likely to be a
    problem

                  Monitoring hole conditions


Critical decisions in the drilling of a well
are made based on what the wellsite team
determines is happening downhole.
Judgment is based on interpretation of all
the available source of information.
Monitoring key drilling parameters and
their variation vs. predicted values can
effectively minimize the number of stuck
pipe incidents.
The following key elements will be
presented in this section:

  Torque

   Overpull

   Circulating pressure

  Pump strokes

   Differential pressure

   Penetration rate

  Mud rheology

  ECD

  Downhole weight on bit and torque

  Cavings and cuttings

  Hole cleaning

   Pick up (P/U), Slack Off (S/O)

   Weights

                 Monitoring hole conditions

  Torque


An increase in torque does not necessarily
result in an increase of drag, and vice
versa. In other words, T&D are not
necessarily interrelated.

An increase in surface torque may indicate
   Inadequate hole cleaning
  Change in the hole angle
   Formation change
   Increase in WOB
   Poor mud properties
   Formation washout
  An increase in lateral vibration
  Tendency for differential sticking

A decrease in surface torque may indicate
   Improved hole cleaning
  The hole straightening out
   Formation change
  Change in the rotary speed
   Decrease in WOB
   Better mud properties
   Bit wearing out
  The positive effect of lubricants

Varying surface torque may indicate
  A rapid change in the sequence of
    formations being drilled
  Reaming with a stabilizer
  A bit balling up
  Junk in the hole
   Dry drilling, i.e., drilling with restricted
    or no circulation
   Stick–slip and vibrations

                 Monitoring hole conditions

  Overpull

Overpull trends are affected by many
factors.

An increase in overpull may indicate
   Inadequate hole cleaning
   Cuttings beds
   Hole geometry change
   Formation change
   Poor mud properties
   Tight spots, such as undergauge hole
    or thicker mudcake

A decrease in overpull may indicate
   Improved hole cleaning
   Formation change
   Better mud properties
  The positive effect of lubricants
  The hole straightening out
   Out-of-gauge hole sections

                  Monitoring hole conditions

  Circulating pressure

The circulating pressure, or pump
pressure, represents all the pressure
losses in the circulating system. By
monitoring changes in circulating
pressure, it is possible to identify a range
of downhole issues.

A circulating pressure increase may
indicate
   Flow rate increase
   Restriction in the annulus
   Restriction inside the drillstring
   Inadequate hole cleaning
  Change in the mud properties: an
    increase in mud density or viscosity
   Plugged or partially plugged bit.

A circulating pressure decrease may
indicate
   Flow rate reduction
  Change in the mud properties: lighter
    or thinner mud
   Lighter fluid entering the wellbore, e.g.,
   an influx
   Washout, either in the drillstring or the
    surface system
   Air in the mud
  Pump malfunction
  Washout in the pipe.
   Lost bit nozzle

A varying pump pressure may indicate
hole cleaning problems.

                   Monitoring hole conditions

 Pump strokes

The pump strokes define the rate at which
mud is pumped into the wellbore. By
monitoring any changes in pump strokes,
it is possible to identify a range of
downhole issues.


An increase in pump strokes, while the
pump stroke rate remains at the same
setting, may indicate

  Change in the mud properties: lighter
    or thinner mud
   Lighter fluid entering the wellbore, e.g.,
   an influx
  Washout in the pipe
   Air in the mud
  Pump malfunction
   Stroke sensor failure

A decrease in pump strokes, while the
pump stroke rate remains at same setting,
may indicate

   Restriction in the annulus
   Restriction inside the drillstring
   Inadequate hole cleaning
  Change in the mud properties: an
    increase of mud density or viscosity
   Plugged or partially plugged bit.
   Stroke sensor failure

                    Monitoring hole conditions

  Penetration rate

The rate of penetration (ROP) is the speed
at which the drill bit can break the rock. By
monitoring changes in the ROP, it is
possible to identify a range of downhole
issues. Note that many factors can initiate
a change in the ROP and other
considerations must be taken into account,
such as flow rate, rotational speed, and
WOB.

The ROP may increase when
   There is a formation change, e.g., to a
     soft formation
    Drilling is close to the balance point
      (i.e., where the mud hydrostatic
    pressure just balances the formation
    pressure).

The ROP may decrease when
  The bit is worn out
  The WOB, rotary speed, or hydraulics
    are no longer optimized
   There is a formation change, e.g., to a
    hard formation
  The mud weight is too high relative to
    the formation borehole pressure
   Poor mud properties affect cuttings
    removal at the bit face and hole
    cleaning
  The bit is balled up.

The ROP may vary when
    Drilling through layered formations
    (intercalations)
   There is inconsistent application of the
  WOB
  The bit is in stick–slip mode.

                    Monitoring hole conditions

 Mud properties monitoring

Several times a day a full check on the
mud will be performed from the mud pit
(suction pit) or flow line to see if the mud’s
properties fall within the planned range. It
is important to observe the trends in mud
properties, as they can give an indication
of the onset of problems. The important
properties to be checked at the mud pit
are described below.

Weight
Although an increase in mud weight
increases the buoyancy and marginally
increases the ability of the mud to lift the
cuttings off the low side of the hole, this
should, however, be balanced against the
other effects of drilling with a high mud
weight, such as the risk of differential
sticking and lost circulation. Higher mud
density also results in lower ROP.

Mud density may increase when
  The drilling solids increase
  A heavy spot from barite sag or
    improper mixing is encountered
  The mud has been over weighted at
    surface.

Mud density may decrease when
   Water, oil, or gas enters the wellbore
   Water, oil or diesel lubricant is added
  The mud is over diluted at surface.

                   Monitoring hole conditions

 Mud properties monitoring

Funnel Viscosity
Measured using a Marsh funnel, the funnel
viscosity is a simple qualitative measure of
mud thickness. The mud funnel viscosity
may increase when
   Reactive drilled solids are present
  The drilling solids increase
  The mud is dehydrated
   There is calcium contamination from
    the cement
   There is anhydrite or salt
    contamination
The mud funnel viscosity may decrease
when
   Formation water enters the wellbore
   There is excessive water content
   There has been excessive treatment
    with thinners.

Plastic viscosity (PV)
Measured using a Fann viscometer, the
PV is a measure of the force required to
keep the drilling fluid moving once it has
started to flow. It depends on the size,
shape, and quantity of solids in the mud.
An increase in PV may indicate solids
buildup in the mud.
The mud PV may increase when
   Unconsolidated sand is drilled
  The quantity of low-gravity solids
   (LGS) increases because of poor
    solids control
   Is the mud has a low water content,
    possibly due to evaporation
The mud PV may decrease when
   Formation water enters the wellbore
    with WBM
  The mud is being diluted with solids
    free mud or the base fluid
  The solids content decreases.

                    Monitoring hole conditions

 Mud properties monitoring

Yield point (YP)
Measured with a Fann viscometer, the YP
is a measure of the force required to get
the drilling fluid to start flowing from
stationary.

The mud YP may increase when
   Reactive solids are present
  The mud is over treated with
     viscosifier
  The mud has a low base fluid content
   There is chemical contamination from
    the formation (salt or calcium, for
    example)
The mud YP may decrease when
   Formation water enters the wellbore
  Too much water has been added
  The mud is over treated with thinners.

Gel strength
Gel strength is an indication of the
attractive forces between particles in a
static situation. Gel strength is measured
using a Fann viscometer. Two
measurements: at 10 sec and at 10 min.

Mud gel strength may increase when
   Reactive formations are drilled
   There is a low water content
    (evaporation)
   Calcium from cement contaminates
    the mud
   There is chemical contamination from
    the formation (anhydrite, for example).
Mud gel strength may decrease when
   Formation water enters the formation
  Too much base fluid has been added
   Chemical thinners are added.

                    Monitoring hole conditions

 Mud properties monitoring

Methylene blue test (MBT)
Performed to determine the amount of
reactive clay in WBM. High reactive clay
may lead to clay balls, shakers blinding, bit
balling. Reactive shale content should be
less than 25lb/bbl bentonite equivalent and
decrease as the hole size reduces.

Solids content
Solids content, especially low gravity
solids (LGS) is critical when using a rig
with limited hydraulics capabilities. LGS
impacts PV and pump pressure.
Recommended LGS values:
  <10% in OBM
  <6% in WBM

Sand content
Recommended <1% to minimize
equipment wear. Use fine mesh screens,
desanders and dilution to lower sand
content.

Mud cake
Thin and strong mud cake is desirable to
minimize differential sticking incidents.
API HP-HT fluid loss in OBM should be
kept < 5 cm3 and < 5–8 cm3 for WBM

Mudcake may increase when
  The LGS increases
   Dilution with water is excessive
  The mud is undertreated with fluid loss
    reducer
   Fluid loss increases for any reason

Mudcake may decrease when
  The mud is adequately treated
   Lost circulation material is added.

                    Monitoring hole conditions

 ECD monitoring

The APWD tool provides real-time
measurements of ECD and equivalent
static density (ESD). When the data is
complemented with surface parameters, it
enables the well site to improve drilling
practices for hole cleaning, borehole
stability, and well control.

Note that the tool picks up changes in
ECD caused by the amount of cuttings
suspended in the mud.
In deviated wells cuttings lay on the low
side of the hole and not in the mud stream,
therefore the APWD may not detect a hole
cleaning problem.
Monitoring torque and drag is a more
effective way to evaluate hole cleaning
conditions in highly deviated wells.

If an APWD tool is run, the following
should be done:
  The ECD value should be plotted
    against the theoretical.
  The data from the tool should be used
     in conjunction with surface trend
    analysis, e.g., T&D.
  The wellsite should be aware of its
    limitations and should not rely on
  APWD data 100%.
  An experienced operator should be
    on-site to analyze the data.

ESD data can be transmitted up hole in
near real time giving a downhole
measurement of LOT’s and connection
pressures. This allows tighter control of
downhole pressure.

                   Monitoring hole conditions

 Downhole WOB and torque

Having DWOB and DTOR measurements
in the BHA allows us to:
  Measure the actual weight applied to
    the bit to enable ROP optimization
  Measure the downhole torque to
    provide a clear picture of the downhole
    conditions and differentiate torque
    from along the string
   Differentiate between formation
   changes by recognizing those with
    poor drilling efficiency
   Eliminate unnecessary trips
   Detect sticking problems to minimize
    stuck pipe incidents
   Identify areas of poor performance by
    distinguishing between drillstring
    effects (hanging on ledges, high
     friction coefficients and BHA vibration)
   and bit effects.
DWOB can also be used as a measure of
hole cleaning in a deviated hole. When
hole cleaning is deteriorating, the
downhole data deviates from the surface
values, e.g., the rig weight indicator shows
the WOB is 30 klbs (or 27 t), but the
downhole data indicates that the downhole
WOB is only 10 klbs (or 4.5 t). Drag is a
better measure of this.

  Hole cleaning

Rotary speed
Rotary speed is critical. Field experience
has shown that rotary speeds of >120 rpm
(and also at >180 rpm) dramatically
increase the rate of removal of cuttings in
large-diameter deviated wells drilled in
laminar flow.





The table below shows the “rule of thumb”
for rotary speed in high angle holes:
 Hole size         Rotary speed
               Big DP’s    Small DP’s
 17.5”        120        120
 12.25”       120        120
 9.875”       60         120
 8.5”         60         120
 6.5”         60         120

Keep in mind that well path design,
directional drilling strategy, and BHA
design play key roles in determining how
fast the drillstring can be rotated without
causing other problems (such as casing
wear, excessive drill pipe or BHA fatigue,
and/or shock and vibration problems). All
these factors are to be considered during
planning phase.

  Hole cleaning

Low-end mud rheology
Low-end rheology (3- and 6-rpm readings)
helps cuttings removal by increasing the
viscous coupling. The table below contains
the general rule of thumb target values:

 Hole Ø  6-rpm reading x Hole size
 <12.25”   1.0-1.2 x hs (hole cleaning)
            0.8-1.0 x hs (manage ECD)
 >12.25”  1.0 x hole size (hole
            cleaning)





It is important to strike a balance with the
low-end rheology such that the mud is
neither too thin nor too thick.
If the fluid is too thick:
     It will be more prone to channeling up
    the high side of the hole, against
    effective cuttings removal
     It may increase pumping pressures
   and ECD obliging to reduce flow rate

If the mud is too thin:
  The fluid will lose its viscous coupling
   and will not be able to move cuttings
   as efficiently with rotation.
   Cuttings will drop out the fluid more
     easily, which makes hole cleaning
    slower.

 Recommended practices -
  Circulating

A clean hole is defined as a wellbore with
a cuttings bed height and distribution such
that operations are trouble free.
A clean hole to trip a BHA may be not
clean enough to run a casing.
Circulation while drilling:
   Monitor the shakers for formation
    cuttings returns. A high ROP should
    generate a high volume of cuttings. If
     this is not happening, stop drilling and
    circulate the hole clean before
    continuing.
     If the volume of cuttings coming to
    surface does not correspond to the
   ROP, cuttings are accumulating in the
    well and will most likely cause
    problems.
  Do not drill faster than you can
    effectively clean the well!

Circulation before connection:
   Circulate hole clean for at least 5-
   10minutes using normal drilling flow
    rate
   Maintain rotary speed according to
    inclination and hole size to give
    effective hole cleaning
   After connection break the gels by
    rotating first then stage up pumps
    slowly to avoid pressure surges
  Never rotate and break circulation
    at same time!
  When returns are observed on
    surface, increase the pump rate and
    rotation to the recommended drilling
    rates.

 Recommended practices -
  Connection

Sticking problems can occur when making
connections regardless of hole size. The
following guidelines, which assume top-
drive drilling, should be used to minimize
potential problems during connections:
   Connections should only be made if
    the hole condition is good. Never
   make a connection with any overpull
    into the slips.
  Wipe at least the last joint before
   making a connection. If erratic or high
    torque is experienced before the
    connection, take time to ensure the
    cuttings are well above the BHA.
  Keep connection time to a minimum.
   After making a connection, and
    starting rotation, break circulation
    slowly while checking for returns.
   Avoid sudden starting or stopping of
    the mud pumps. This may adversely
    affect wellbore stability because of the
    shock-loading effect.
     If differential sticking is expected to be
   a risk:
       maximize the pipe motion
       consider rotating the drillstring with
        rotary table while picking up the
        next stand.
      Perform a risk assessment
       before attempting this
       procedure.
       record and plot the static torque
          friction and compare it with the
       dynamic off-bottom torque. An
        increase in static friction relative to
       dynamic friction is an early
        indicator of differential sticking
       tendency.
     If hole conditions are sticky, extra
    stick-up may be required.

 Recommended practices -
  Connection

Connection practices in highly deviated
and extended-reach wells should differ
from those in vertical or low-angle wells.
The recommended baseline connection
procedure is as follows:
 1.   Drill down the stand at the current
      rotational speed and flow rate.
 2.  Pick up off-bottom and increase the
     flow rate and the rotational speed to
      their maximum.
 3. Ream one stand out and back in;
     repeat if the hole is tight.
 4.  Acquire the off-bottom torque and
      drillstring weight data.
 5.  Shut down the rotary system.
 6.  Reciprocate the pipe and obtain the
     pickup and slack-off weights.
 7.  Shut down the pumps and make the
     connection.
 8.  After connection record break over
     torque on starting rotation.
The aims of this connection procedure are
to
  move cuttings away from the BHA to
    ensure trouble-free connection
   condition the new section of hole that
   has been drilled
   collect P/U, S/O and ROT weight data
     in a consistent manner (fill in the
    broomstick chart).

                Recommended practices

 Recommended practices -
  Tripping

Most stuck pipe events occur while
tripping, especially in deviated wells where
hole cleaning is an issue. It is, therefore,
imperative that trips are planned and
monitored. Unnecessary trips should be
avoided.
Considerations before tripping:
   the hole will never be 100% clean,
   even when circulating at the optimum
    parameters
  a trip log identifying previous trouble
    spots should be prepared.
   have a contingency plan if a tight spot
     is encountered
  communicate an overpull limit in
    writing to the driller
   optimize the mud system to facilitate
     tripping, i.e. adding lubricants,
    conditioning mud for casing running,
   and cementing.

Considerations during tripping:
  Have a pre-tripping meeting.
   Circulate hole clean with rotation until
    the shakers are clean before POOH
  Be patient and continue circulating if
    cuttings still coming over the shakers.
   Record the depth of the top of the BHA
    while circulating bottoms-up before
     tripping. Take extreme care when the
    top stabilizer reaches this depth and
     for the following two stands, as this is
   where it is likely that the BHA will be
    pulled into a cuttings bed, if one exists.
   Monitor the pills coming out of the hole
   and record the amount of cuttings.
  Measure and record the average hook
    load every stand. Plot the data and
   use the trend to determine if the
     drillstring is being pulled into an
    obstruction.

                Recommended practices

 Recommended practices –
  Tight spot/Cuttings bed
 procedure

Considerations during tripping:
 1.  Note the depth of the tight spot.
 2. Do not force the drillstring out of the
     hole using excessive overpull.
 3.  Run opposite to the direction of the
       trip at least 2-3 stands.
 4.  Rotate the pipe at 30–40 rpm to
     break the gels around the pipe.
 5.  Bring the pumps on at a low rate until
     returns are seen.
 6.  Increase the pump rate to the drilling
     rate and start rotation,
 7.  Rotate as fast as the hole cleaning
    recommendations.
 8.  Circulate for 30 minutes with a high
     flow rate and rotary speed.
 9.  Shut down the pumps and
    commence the trip again on
     elevators. If the tight spot is
    encountered shallower in the well,
      this confirms that the tight spot is
     cuttings related. At this stage, trip
    back in the hole 3-5 stands and
    recommence cleanup operations; this
     time for as long as necessary to
     clean the hole.
 10. Pull out of hole with caution; if the
      tight spot is still there, this may
     indicate a mechanical hole problem.

 Recommended practices –
 Reaming

If reaming operations are conducted too
fast, solids from washouts and cavings
can be introduced into the circulating
system faster than the hole is being
cleaned and may result in a pack off.
General reaming practices:
   Wellsite supervisor and directional
     driller should always supervise these
    operations
  Use same or similar BHA including
     stabilizer size and bit type
  Back reaming features are
   recommended on bit and stabilizers
  Use consistent parameters while
   reaming to easily identify changes in
    torque and pressure trends
   Circulate out the extra material (i.e.
   new cavings) introduced in the hole
   Monitor torque and drag and pressure
    trends and circulate hole clean if
    indications of pack off occur. Be ready
    to drop the flow rate if hole packs off
    to avoid over pressuring the well.
   Adjust reaming speed and circulation
    time according to returning cuttings
   volume
  Reaming should be done with drilling
    flow rate and similar mud properties
  Keep weight on bit and speed alarp to
    minimize risk of accidental sidetrack
  Use caution when reaming soft
    formations

                Recommended practices

 Recommended practices –
 Back reaming

The following problems are seen when
back reaming high angle wells:
  A cuttings dune builds up just above
    the BHA posing significant risk of pack
     off and stuck pipe
     If a pack off occurs there is a risk to
    permanently damaging the wellbore
   below it (hydraulic hammer)
     If back reaming is too fast it may
    induce higher ECD, lost circulation,
    ballooning, wellbore instability
   problems and pack off
  Back reaming can aggravate casing
   wear due to increased tension forces
  Back reaming may induce higher
    vibration and drillstring fatigue failure.
    Monitor tool shock data
  Time consuming operation: once
    started back reaming cannot be
    stopped until inside casing at least
  Pump out or back reaming with
     insufficient rotation may induce as well
   pack off
When not to back ream?
Back reaming should be avoided as first
action when:
   tripping out of the hole is possible on
    the elevators
   there are pre-existing fractures or
     failed rocks, and there is a risk of
    wellbore instability
   cuttings beds exist in high-angle wells
     (risk of pack off)
   the hole is not clean, especially in
    deviated holes
   the rig pumps have insufficient
    capacity for adequate hole cleaning
   the mud weight and condition are
    inadequate
   casing wear is a concern.

 Recommended practices –
  Acquire T&D readings

1.  After drilling down each connection,
   work the stand once to ensure any
    cuttings are clear of the BHA.
2.  Obtain the rotating weight and the
    torque at drilling rotational speed and
    flow rate, 1-2m off bottom.
3.  Stop rotating and obtain the pickup
    weight on the upstroke.
4.  Acquire the slack-off weight on the
   down stroke while returning to
    bottom.
5.   If necessary, acquire the pumps-off
    pickup and slack-off weights; stop the
   pumps and repeat steps 3 and 4
   above before the connection.
6.  Work the drillstring at the same
   speed every time. It will make the
    readings more consistent.
7. Do not take the average readings at
    connections. Record peak and a
    steady reading after overcoming the
      initial static friction and pipe stretch.
8.  Also take the circulating readings at
    the same flow rate for each hole
    section to avoid the potential
    influence/interference of hydraulic lift.
9.  Pumps-off readings at connections
    are preferable, as they give a truer
    representation of the friction factor
   and the expected readings while
     tripping.
10. While tripping out, just obtain the
    pickup weights while pulling; for
    tripping in, acquire the slack-off
    weights while running. Document the
    depths and numbers of any abnormal
    pickup or slack-off values.

Identifying and freeing stuck pipe




  Once a stuck pipe incident has occurred,
   understanding the mechanism of the
   event is fundamental to take the correct
   remedial action. Misunderstanding
   sticking mechanisms and/or
   inappropriate first actions can worsen
  many stuck pipe incidents. Be aware and
  make client aware that actions to free the
   pipe can lead to damage to our tools.

   In the event of a stuck pipe, the following
   steps should be undertaken:
    1.  Evaluate the sticking mechanism
      and employ the most appropriate
           first action without delay.
    2.   If this does not free the pipe,
       employ a secondary freeing action
          (for example, pumping pills) and
        follow it with a series of jarring
        operations.
    3.   If these actions do not work, a
        backoff operation should be
       considered and fishing should be
          tried. How long the freeing
        operation takes depends on the
       economics of fishing.

Dealing with overpull
The golden rule when excessive overpull
is observed during tripping out is to
assume that the sticking is a hole cleaning
related issue.
The first actions to take during tripping out
are to:
    1.  Run back in hole for at least two
        or three stands
    2.  Switch on the pumps and bring
        the flow rate up in stages to the
      maximum rate. Rotate the
          drillstring at maximum speed.
    3.  Circulate for at least one bottoms
      up at maximum flow rate and
         rotation speed.
    4.  Monitor the shakers. If large
      amounts of cuttings are coming
        over the shakers, circulate until
        the shakers are clean. Consider
       pumping sweeps if necessary.
    5.  Stop pumping.
    6.  Pull past the original stuck point. If
      no overpull is encountered, the
         situation was hole-cleaning
         related. If the same overpull is
       observed, it is probably a
       mechanical or geometry-related
       problem, and appropriate action
       needs to be taken, for example,
       back reaming.

                Identifying and freeing stuck pipe

  Solid induced stuck pipe
  freeing procedure

First action
The main objective is to reestablish
circulation if it has become impossible or
restricted.

 1.   If the drillstring starts to torque up
    and is about to pack off, reduce the
    pump strokes by half to minimize the
     trapped pressure. If the hole cleans
     up, return the flow to the normal rate.
 2.   If the string packs off, stop the
    pumps immediately and bleed down
     the standpipe pressure (ported float
     valve required). When bleeding
     pressure down from under a pack off,
     control the rate so that solids are not
    U-tubed into the drillstring, as they
     might plug the string.
 3.  Leave low pressure (<500 psi) trapped
    below the pack off. This will indicate
     that the situation is improving if the
     pressure starts to bleed off. Do not
    exceed this trapped pressure, as you
     could break the formation or compact
     the pack off.
 4.  With the drillstring hanging at its free
     rotating weight, start cycling the string
    up to maximum makeup torque. Do not
    work the string up or down at this time.
 5.  Continue cycling the torque while
     watching for pressure bleed off and
     returns at the shakers. If bleed off or
      partial circulation occurs, slowly
     increase the pump strokes to maintain
    a maximum standpipe pressure of 500
      psi. If circulation improves, continue
     to increase the pump strokes.

                Identifying and freeing stuck pipe

   Differential sticking freeing
  procedure

First action
 1.  Establish that differential sticking is the
    mechanism. This will usually be
     indicated by factors such as sticking
      after a connection or after a survey
    where there was unrestricted
      circulation across a permeable
     formation (sandstone, dolomite, and
     possibly limestone).
 2.   If pumping, drop the flow rate to
    reduce the effective mud weight and
     attempt to move the string.
 3.  Circulate at the maximum allowable
     flow rate in an effort to erode the
    mudcake.
     a.  In slim hole conditions, this might
       be impossible owing to equivalent
          circulating density constraints (the
        overbalance will increase).
     b.   It might not be effective in
          directional wells where the pipe is
        stuck on the low side and the
      maximum fluid velocity is on the
        high side.
      c.  Stop circulation to remove ECD
          effect
 4.  Slump the string while holding 50% of
     the makeup torque of the surface pipe,
     (unless a mixed string of pipe is being
     used). Use an action similar to that
    used with a bumper sub to work the
     torque down.
     a.  Ensure that the torque can be
        released gradually without backing
         out a downhole connection.
     b.  Pulling the pipe could create more
         side force, which pulls the pipe
        harder into the mudcake, and it
         also reduces the amount of torque
         that can be applied.

will provide an indication as to when the
pipe is free. Prepare enough HCl for a
second soaking if needed. A 10–30-bbl
spacer of water or diesel should be used
in front of and behind the HCl solution.
An appropriate acid inhibitor should be
used to prevent possible corrosion of the
drill pipe.
After the pipe is free, the HCl solution can
be displaced. If the HCl has been
completely depleted, it can be
incorporated into the mud; the mud’s pH
should then be adjusted. If the HCl is not
depleted, the solution should be disposed
if possible, and pH adjusted (lime or
caustic soda) quickly to reduce
contamination.
There are safety precautions to be
observed when using HCl:
   Dilute concentrated acid by adding it
    to water. NEVER add water to acid.
   Circulate HCl solution out through the
   choke at a slow pump rate, as carbon
    dioxide gas may be present after the
    acid has reacted with carbonate
    formations.
  Use proper safety equipment when
    handling HCl.
   Maintain enough caustic soda and
    lime on location to neutralize the acid
    solution when it is circulated out of the
    hole.
