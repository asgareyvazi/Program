1      Introduction and Background

Prior to start of drilling operations on SP678, Statoil issued “Reservoir Development Plan for
South Pars phases 6, 7 & 8” (February 2004). The document described the geological and
reservoir simulation model. Changes made to the original MDP (Master Development Plan)
were documented and various sensitivities were run to study various effects and document
recommendations. The main recommendations from February 2004 were:

   o  Include K1 in the field development
   o  Open hole completion, with predrilled liner
   o No initial stimulation or perforation
   o  Drill and complete the wells to approximately 2000 m horizontal displacement with an
        inclination of 50° at TD
   o  Wells to be drilled to approximately 50 meters above Gas Water Contact (GWC)


Changes from the MDP were discussed and approved by POGC and the JMC (Joint
Management Committee).

All production wells in South Pars phase 6,7 and 8 are tested and the test results display
satisfactory production capacity and meet the requirements stated in the MDP. The new wells
are able to produce in excess of the MDP requirement.

All the 27 new wells are open for production from all the four reservoir sections, K1, K2, K3
and K4. This will maximize the ultimate recovery of gas and condensate. The chosen
predrilled liner / openhole completion concept proved cost efficient compared to the cemented
liner concept. The concept is also expected to be the main reason for the high productivity.

This document summarize the results from the 27 new production wells drilled on phases 6, 7
and 8 and the three appraisal well that were tied back as gas producers. An updated simulation
reference case is made and some sensitivities are also shown.

2     Geology, Geophysics and Petrophysics


2.1     Introduction

The late Permian Dalan Formation and the early Triassic Kangan Formation are the main gas
reservoir on the South Pars Field. Oil is discovered in the more shallow Cretaceous
Formations. Kangan and Dalan Formation are divided into five major sequences called Upper
Kangan and Khuff 1, 2, 3 & 4 (K1, K2, K3 &K4).

Khuff consists mainly of dolomites, limestone and minor anhydrites. Seven major depositional
environments are identified, spreading from evaporitic/sabkhas flats to offshore settings.

The Top Reservoir Map have been updated based on the results from the 27 production wells
that have been drilled, in addition some more appraisal wells have been included in the depth
conversion. The petrophysical parameters have been updated based on the results from the new
wells.


2.2     General Regional Geology and Setting

In the late Early Permian, the Arabian Plate is interpreted to have undergone a major phase of
crustal extension resulting in continental separation. Progressive thermal uplift is interpreted to
have occurred along the present-day Zagros Suture culminating in the regional ‘pre Khuff
unconformity’. In the Late Permian, continental spreading took place as the Neo-Tethys Ocean
started to form.

Movement on major faults produced substantial, thickness variations within the Lower Dalan.
Movement on these faults ceased, however, following the deposition of the base middle
anhydrite. A major shallow water carbonate platform sedimentation subsequently prevailed
across the entire Arabian Plate, lasting until the late Toarcian.

KANGAN FORMATION (thickness 180-188 m)

Upper Kangan (thickness 30 m).
The uppermost part of the Kangan consists of tight dolomites interbedded with claystone and
anhydrite. The claystone are clearly seen on the logs because of the high gamma ray values.
Upper Kangan is not considered as reservoir. The 9 5/8” casing is set in Upper Kangan.

K 1  (thickness 107-115)
K1 is divided into reservoir zone K1U, K1M and K1L. The top K1 is picked below a thin
anhydrite layer that are identified in all production wells on phases 6, 7 & 8.

Reservoir zone K1U, the topmost 23-37 m of the sequence, consists of dolomite with some
minor mudstone and anhydrite stringers. This uppermost section is generally tight although
thin stringer with some better reservoir properties appears.

Reservoir zone K1M which are about 50 meters thick consists predominantly of grainstone,
mudstone and wackestone with subordinate dolomite stringers. Anhydrite nodules appear in
the uppermost and lowermost part of the zone. Reservoir zone K1M is regarded as the
productive part of K1, with an average porosity of 13 %. The average permeability varies
highly from about 10 mD in some wells and up to 240 mD estimated on one well on phase 6.
The reservoir properties are best developed in phase 6 and 7 while K1M seems to be generally
poor in phase 8.

Reservoir zone K1L consists of dolomitized grainstone, wackestone and mudstone. Anhydrite
appears as isolated nodules and as stringers at the base of the section. K1L has a very low
permeability, in average around 1 mD.


K 2  (thickness 42-45 m)
K2 is divided into reservoir zone K2U, K2M and K2L. The base of K2L is interpreted to
represent the base of the Triassic sequence. The two uppermost are the better one and almost
identical in the three appraisal wells SP-9, SP-10 and SP-11. Reservoir zone K2M consists of
some porous dolomite and limestone with good reservoir properties whilst K2U and K2L seem
to be tight with average permeabilities below
4 mD.



DALAN FORMATION

K 3 (thickness 119-122)
The K3 member marks the top of the Permian sequence on South Pars. K3 is divided into three
reservoir layers, K3U, K3M and K3L respectively.

Reservoir zone K3U with an average thickness off 21 meters consists of porous grainstone,
mainly dolomite, minor limestone with a gradational base into tight rock. In phase 6 this zone
shows generally good reservoir properties with permabilities up to 138 mD while the well in
phase 7 and 8 shows generally lower values.

Reservoir zone K3M is about 88 meters thick and consists of tight dolomite, some limestone at
the base. This zone is generally tight but thin permeable stringers exist and these are allowed to
produce since the wells are completed by open hole and not a cemented liner.

Reservoir zone K3L consists interbedded dolomite and limestone, predominantly wackestone
but also some packstone and grainstone. Some thin layers within K3L have very good reservoir
properties, however tight anhydrite stringers occur at the base of K3, marking the K4
boundary.

Khuff 4 (thickness 149-152 m)
The K4 is divided into three reservoir layers, K4U, K4M and K4L. The two uppermost
reservoir zones are regarded as the most productive one. K4L was not penetrated by the
production wells. TD of the production wells is in K4M.

Reservoir zone K4U consists of dolomite and some limestone and have good porosities.
However the permeability is lower than expected because of the oomoldic texture (isolated
pores). Average permeabilities for the zone vary between
30-290 mD.

Reservoir zone K4M consists of oolitic limestone, predominantly grainstone but both
wackestone and mudstone layers becomes more common closer to the base. Average
permeabilities variy between 25-100 mD.

The upper most part of reservoir zone K4L is defined by a 1-5 meters thick mudstone to
wackestone, and represents a widespread flooding surface. K4L is a major unit of porous
grainstone, interbedded with wackestone and mudstone. The lowermost part of K4L consists
predominantly of dolomite with a high proportion of grainstone, but transitional from good to
poor rock properties below. Cementation by anhydrite increases towards the base of K4L.

2.5     Gas-Water-Contact (GWC)


The interpretation of the GWC contact has not changed as a result of the new production wells.
The wells has not penetrated the GWC contact, they are stopped around 50 m above the
contact.

The gas water contact on South Pars varies over the field, it is deepest in southeast, defined by
SP-5 (GDT at 3185 m TVD), and the most shallow are observed in SP-4 (GDT at 2783 m
TVD, WUT 2816). This indicates a depth difference of around 400 meters from east to west
over a distance of approximately 45 km.

The contact-points are not always easy to pick and are often defined as “gas down to” (GDT).
In six of the wells (SP-3, SP-4, SP-5, SP-6, SP-10 & SP-11) GDT are defined, while four of
the wells (SP-2, SP-7 & SP-9) have a well-defined gas water contact. Some of the wells have a
somewhat uncertain contact like in SP-1, SP-8 and SP-12. The two latter are uncertain because
of limited data. Statoil have limited information from phases 2 & 3 where Total has finalized
24 production wells. Statoil are told that the contact is defined by GDT in the lowermost and
tight parts of K4 in most of the wells in phases 2 & 3. This fits with the contact seen in SP-6
and also the GWC-surface that is modelled. Well SP13 has been drilled in the south east, in
phase 12. The model has not been updated with respect to SP13.

2.6     Petrophysics

After drilling all wells Phases 6, 7 & 8, the available petrophysical data were reviewed and
reservoir parameters recalculated. In addition, standard core plug porosities for all the three
appraisal wells have become available, thus providing the possibility to check the log
evaluation results.


2.6.1   Appraisal Wells

Log derived porosity values were checked against the available core plug results and found
generally in good agreement. New averages of reservoir parameters for each zone were
calculated from the wireline log results. The K-4 Lower values remained unchanged.


2.6.2   Production Wells

All production wells were logged with LWD only. Triple Combo memory data, consisting of
GR, Resistivity and Density/Neutron, is available for all 8.5” reservoir sections. The sensors
were located behind the motor and stabilizer parts of the BHA.

The Density/Neutron sensors provide the most important data set, since the main lithology and
porosity is calculated from this response. The logs were recorded within one to two hours after
bit penetration, thus suffering from gas effect on the log response.

The Neutron log is in general of good quality and the response is similar to the wireline logs,
acquired in the appraisal wells.

The PEF curve, in many cases the only lithology indicator, was severely affected by tool
standoff and hole enlargements. The raw curve was edited in all sections, before it was used in
the formation evaluation.

The available resistivity logs do not provide a mud invasion profile and are of limited accuracy
concerning vertical resolution and high resistivity response, thus the calculated water
saturation is within a high uncertainty range.

A basic determination of Porosity, Water Saturation and Net/Gross, was performed using the
Interactive Petrophysics software package, as it is described in the Final Well Reports. The
original interpretation and the cut-off values of 2 P.U. and 0.8 SW were maintained. To
account for uncertainty in the Dolomite/Limestone distribution and thus total porosity, the
average porosity values for the K-2 Upper, K-3 Upper & Lower and the K-4 Upper layers were
increased by maintaining the original N/G and saturation values.

3     Well Performances


3.1     Introduction

In this chapter the well clean-up and test operations are described. The main well performance
data are presented.


3.2     Completion

In all new wells the completion was run immediately after drilling the 8,5” reservoir section.
A 7” predrilled liner was run in mud with a preinstalled barrier plug in the top of the liner. The
wells were then displaced to 1.00 SG packer fluid before the production packer and 7”
production tubing was installed in two separate run.

After having installed the X-mas tree, a high pressure Coflexip flowline was hooked up to the
production wing valve and the barrier plug was retrieved on slickline. This Baker AORH prong
and plug required two slickline runs to be fully retrieved.

With the well filled with packer fluid, there was a differential pressure of approximately 90 bar
acting below the plug. To maintain well control, 100 - 110 bars were kept on the well at all
time from releasing the plug until the wells were opened for flow.

For the two first wells (SPD7-02 & SPD7-15) a pressure cycle valve was used in bottom of
well in stead of wireline plugs. These valves did not operate as expected and the concept was
changed to the AORH -prong and plug system. Thereby slickline operation was introduced.

3.4    Kick start

The hydrostatic head of the packer fluid (inhibited fresh water at 1,00 SG), result in shut
in a surface pressure of around 90 bars. In practice the shut in wellhead pressure was
kept at 100 – 110 bar prior to opening the wells to flow. This was to maintain
overbalance, thus avoid influx of gas prior to “Kick-start”.

To maximize the instantaneous pressure drop in the wellbore, it was important to avoid
any gas influx prior to opening the well for flow.

Production started by utilizing the “Kick-start” principle. By opening the wells
instantaneously on a gate valve on the well test choke manifold and keeping both the
adjustable and fixed chokes open at maximum bean size, the objective was to bled down
the wellhead pressure to zero so that a maximum pressure wave could be sent down to
entire pay zone. Then, rapidly establishing minimum achievable bottomhole pressure.
This procedure was conducted to unload the packer fluid and maximum amount of mud
from the well bore.

The wells were produced at maximum rate until surface pressure reached 50-100 bar, or
until gas approached surface. Gas to surface took typically 6-12 minutes which proves
that initial flowrates were very high.

A few wells did not achieve optimal kick start mainly due to various operational
difficulties.

When the liquid – gas interface reached surface, the production was choked back to
reduce the strain on surface lines and reduce the erosion potential. Then the gas rate was
gradually increased to maximum rate dependent on weather condition or other surface
flow restrictions. Max rates varied between 80 to 150 mill Scf/d. The maximum gas rate
depended on whether both burner booms could be used or not.

3.7     Validity of well performance data

       The reported well performance is based on relatively short, transient flow
        periods in the range of 1 – 12 hours. Consequently there has been a concern
        about the validity of test result being representative for stabilized well
        performance. A comprehensive discussion regarding this concern can be found
         in Appendix E.

         In general, the relative error between the reported flowing pressures based on
         short flow periods and stabilized flowing pressure is within 10% or less of the
       drawdown.

       The wells test data from the South Pars 6,7&8 wells are valid for documenting
         the initial well performance and for comparing well performances amongst
        various wells, because the error between the transient well test data and
         stabilized well performance is negligible. This is because the drawdown is
         relatively small. A comprehensive discussion of this concern is given in
       Appendix E.

4.4     Adjustment of Permeabilities to observed data

Permeabilities are one of the most sensitive parameters when producing gas from South

As shown in chapter 3, productivities in wells are generally high. Actual well

assuming skin was zero, this means that all permabilities needed to be multiplied by 0,8

in the reservoir simulation model. This is based on FWHP and FBHP in the two PLT-
logged wells.

The wells SPD8-6 and SPD9-6 are the two appraisal wells in phase 7 and 8 (SP9 &
SP10). To obtain a match of their performance (rate versus FWHP), they are given skin-

5     Production constraints

This chapter describes the main constraints like capacities and efficiencies for onshore
facilities, pipelines, platforms and wells used in reservoir simulator.


5.1    Onshore Facility and Platform efficiencies

The production rate from all three platforms is 2700 million Scf per calendar day (76,5
mill Sm³/cd). This is based on a stream day rate of 3000 million Scf and expectation of
90 % uptime to compensate for planned or unplanned downtime of land facility,
platforms and pipelines.


5.2     Well Efficiency

In addition to onshore facility, pipeline and platform capacities, wells will be shut down
for workover, maintenance, testing or other services. A common approach is that, on
average, the platform must be able to reach the production target with one well out of
service. For example with 10 wells on a platform, 9 wells should be able to meet the
production target.

Well efficiencies are therefore set to 90 % for all simulations done in this report.

All flow calculations, from reservoir, via pipes to land facility, are then correctly
modelled based on a field rate of 3000 million Scf/d and assuming that 10 % of the well
capacity is unavailable.


5.3     Modelling of pressure drop in wells and pipelines

Dynamic simulations are done with friction loss in the tubing and pipeline. A software
program named PROSPER has been used to generate input to Eclipse (*.VFP files).
Actual tubing geometries are used (length, deviations and diameter).

Flowline lift-curve (from platform to shore) used in a study conducted in February 2004
for the flowlines seems to be too optimistic. Therefore, in this study pressure-drop from
platform to shore is increased by 10 bars to have a more realistic exit-pressure from
platforms.

7      Discussion “Mud left in hole”


7.1    Background

Upon request from POGC and in agreement with Statoil and Petropars, two PLT survey
were run on South Pars 6, 7 & 8. One well on phase 6 (SPD7-09) and one on phase 8
(SPD9-15 were selected for these surveys. They were carried out immediately after
completion and clean-up in October 05 (SPD7-09 on Sagadril-2) and November 05
(SPD9-15 on RaniWoro).

Both wells were logged with Sclumberger MPSP (Memory Production Service Platform).
Log interpretations and analysis was done using the Emeraude-software by Statoil in
Norway. A report summarizing the operations and the results was issued in December
2005.



7.2     Results from PLT

The productivities in the PLT-logged wells are excellent and in accordance with the well
performance from the other production wells on South Pars 6, 7 & 8. These two wells
were cleaned up at gas rates of approximately 85 MMScf/d and produced with
drawdowns of 11-14 bar (180 psi).

For both wells the logged productivity is surprisingly high in the upper part of the pay
zone (K1 and K2). In well (SPD7-09) flow contribution from K1 is approximately 80 %
of total flow. In well SPD9-15, K2 contributes with 80 % of the flow.

These PLT’s show no or only marginal flow contribution from lower levels of the
wellpaths (K3 and K4). This is caused by liquid-overbalance against lower levels during
logging with relative moderate production rates (85/86 MM Scf/d).

Main plots from the two PLT’s are shown on next pages, see figure 7.2.1 and figure
7.2.2.

A four hours build-up was carried out right after flowing for both wells. Information from
the buildup tests are limited but discussed in the PLT-report. Both wells prove high KH-
relationship, even with marginal contribution from K3 and K4.

7.3    Technical discussion

After some time of high and stable production, the wellbore and reservoir will go into a
dynamic equilibrium and so that the entire wellbore can contribute.
Five independent factors support this position.


7.3.1   Increased rates

Well production rates were originally designed for 120 mill Scf/d on SP678. Increasing the
rate from 85 to 120 mill Scf/d will increase the drawdown thus “overcome” the liquid
overbalance and establish underbalance in the lower parts of these wells. More production
from below, will lift fluid out of hole, reducing the overbalance and creating better and better
conditions for the lowermost part of the well. Figure 7.2.1 & 7.2.2 illustrate these phenomena.

As shown in Chapter 3, many wells have been produced at much higher rates compared to the
two PLT-logged wells. Therefore, they are likely to have produced back more, or all of the
mud.


7.3.2   Partly depletion

The active intervals during PLT-logging will deplete over time. The productive intervals that
are not active will locally deplete less. Over time the flowing bottomhole pressure (FBHP) is
reduced in the active intervals. Hence, the drawdown across the lowermost, apparently passive
intervals increase. After some weeks/months of stable production the effective drawdown will
provide production from the entire wellbore.


7.3.3  Mud weight will be reduced to water

The (waterbased) mud left in hole, during PLT-logging varies from 1,2 SG at the top to more
than 2,0 SG at bottom. Since the wells were left shut in, this segregation effect will continue.
Finally the upper part of the liquid column is likely to have a density of 1,1 SG. This effect
will improve the wells ability to unload the liquid phase.

This effect, alone, will reduce the overbalance that was observed during the PLT-logging and
enable the deeper zones to produce when the wells are brought on production again.

7.3.4   Water can segregate into reservoir

The permeability expected to be high at the bottom of the well, and the filtercake will
deteriorate. When the filtercake “fall apart” there is enough permeability for water to segregate
by gravitation into the reservoir and leave only gas (and solids) in the wellbore.

It should be mentioned that when reservoir and flowing bottomhole pressure reaches the dew
point some 2-5 year into the production life, a bottomhole condensate level (and condensed
water) will build up, unless it segregates into the formation.



7.3.5   Favourable well geometry

Wells generally have 40-60° deviation trough the reservoir to TD and are completed with an
open hole liner. If barite settles out, there will still be good communication with reservoir to
TD especially on the high side, above liner.

      Appendix E

       Validity of the well performance data

       With well performance predictions based on relatively short, transient flow periods
         in the range 1 – 12 hrs there will always be some concern about the validity of the
         estimates. General theoretical results have therefore been included in this section
        about the possible error introduced when transient well performance data are used to
        estimate the stabilized well performance. The results are both of specific and general
          interest, and not restricted to cases from the South Pars gas field.

      To get a valid estimate of the well performance one needs to determine the stabilized
        pressure drop for a given rate relative to the average pressure within the drainage
         area. This essentially means flow should exceed the time needed to reach
        pseudosteady state, with decreasing average pressure accounted for through material
        balance considerations. For initial flow tests this approach is normally bypassed by
        using the initial pressure and not the average pressure within the drainage area as
        reference for the drawdown.

       With limited downhole pressures available for use in analyses, the well performance
        data presented in this report have been based on wellhead data. Other than added
        rate-dependent effects, e.g., from friction, the comparisons will be similar. That is,
         the uncertainties in well performance estimates caused by short flow tests will be
         similar, but because of higher total skin values from added rate-dependent effects,
         the errors will actually be reduced.

      As was pointed out above, when flow data are taken from a short, transient flow test
      we normally set the formation (average) pressure equal to the initial pressure and
       assume that Δptrans = pi − p wf (t ) represents the stabilized pressure drop for the
         relevant rate. The challenge now is to relate the transient value Δ ptrans to the correct
         stabilized value Δ p stab = p (t ) − p wf (t ) which will be reached for flow times t ≥ t stab
        with the average pressure p (t ) as reference. For high pressures, e.g., exceeding 210
         bar, we can use the analogy with liquid systems, thus allowing us to use simpler and
        perhaps more familiar identities.

           It is important to emphasize the fact that we are not trying to estimate the drawdown
      Δptrans = pi − p wf (t ) of a correct bounded model at the time needed to reach stabilized
         conditions, but rather the drawdown Δ p stab = p (t ) − p wf (t ) at this time and beyond.
       The difference is important since the drop in average pressure cannot be ignored in
        proper well performance data. To be precise, if data from a stabilized multirate flow
          test is used to determine a single well performance curve, then each data point should
       be determined relative to the current average pressure. The well performance curve
        should then be constructed from the initial pressure with the drawdown at each rate
        used to determine the equivalent flowing pressure at this rate. The latter, correct
        approach might not move the data points significantly, but it does point out the fact
         that when we use transient data we do not need to wait for stabilized flow to get a
         valid estimate of the stabilized drawdown Δp stab . Actually, when the initial pressure
          is used for reference we should not wait that long to estimate Δp stab since we then
       end up overestimating the drawdown. The challenge is to find out how long we
        should wait for any given case.

       The points discussed above can be illustrated with reference to the data in Fig. E1,
         plotted in semilog coordinates, where data from the infinite acting model of Eq. 1 are
        seen to intersect the stabilized lines at just after 30 hours for each of the cases. The
        time needed to reach stabilized flow for the bounded models is roughly equal to 120
        hours for these cases, a factor of about 4 longer. At this flow time the stabilized
       drawdown will be overestimated by roughly the same amount as it will be
        underestimated at a flow time of 12 hours (for these models).

       There is one more important aspect that needs to be discussed for flow-after-flow
          tests. This involves using the effective flow time at the end of each flow period to
       examine the potential error at the relevant rate. The effective flow time is just the
        cumulative production divided by the current rate. Note, however, that the procedure
        of determining effective flow times should be restarted after shut-ins.

       The effect of permeability and size on the time needed to get a good estimate of the
         stabilized drawdown is as expected, with a longer time needed to get a good estimate

         increased. However, with initial pressure as reference, one does not have to wait for

         relatively short flow tests, especially for non-stimulated wells with good formation
         properties. When wellhead data are used to monitor well performance, additional
        pressure losses from friction are likely to add to the total skin effect and thus reduce
         the relative error when data from transient flow tests are used.
